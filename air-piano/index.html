Press Start camera and allow access — you will see yourself, with the key band across the middle. No camera? Click or tap the keys, or play the two keyboard rows (Z row = lower octave, Q row = upper).

How to play

  • Approach silently. Your fingertips show as coloured guide dots everywhere on screen, and a key lights up when a fingertip hovers over it — but moving into position makes no sound. Only the band in the middle can play.
  • Tap down to play. Push a fingertip down across the amber strike line inside a key, the way you'd dip a real key. The note sounds and sustains while your fingertip stays down; lift back above the higher release line (or slide off the key) and it stops. Every fingertip of both hands is its own player — chords and two-handed playing work.
  • How hard you tap is how loud it plays. Loudness comes from your fingertip's fastest downward speed just before the strike, grouped into soft / medium / hard — the band and value print on the key as it fires. Slow drifts into a key stay silent: a press needs a real tap's speed, not just arrival.
  • Modes live above the band. Point your index fingertip at a button along the top and pinch thumb-to-index, holding the pinch about a quarter second — the button fills as the pinch commits. Same buttons work by click, tap, and keyboard focus.
  • Below the band: hold a fist still for a moment to toggle the sustain pedal; hold an open palm still to cut all notes off. Poses are only read below the band, never while you're playing.
  • Mouse / touch: press on a key to play it — the lower on the key you press, the harder the hit. Drag while pressed to glide across keys. Keyboard: Z–M play the lower octave's white keys with S D G H J as its black keys; Q–I and 2 3 5 6 7 do the same an octave up. Hold Shift for a hard hit.

How a press is decided — and the honest limits

The detection recipe is borrowed from the strongest published work, adapted to screen space. A press needs three things at once: the fingertip crosses the key's strike line (~62% down the key), its downward speed peaked above a minimum gate during the approach, and it decelerates as it arrives — the kinematic signature of a deliberate tap. That velocity-gated design (Toshpulatov et al., CVPR 2026) reached 94.4% detection with 4.2% false positives, where position alone managed 70.3% with 27.7% false positives. Entry and exit lines differ (hysteresis), and after a press each finger rests through a 300 ms cooldown before it can fire again — a named constant (PRESS_COOLDOWN_MS) at the top of the script, set toward the low end on purpose because repeated piano notes need it; the contact-detection research used ~450 ms.

Webcam depth is never the deciding signal — a single camera's depth error (12–25 mm) is far coarser than the hover-vs-touch gap (<3 mm). Everything here is screen-space: position, speed, and the tap's shape. Fingertip positions pass through a One Euro filter so a still hand doesn't jitter across a line.

Velocity is the honest weak point. No published webcam air piano has a validated velocity→loudness mapping — the one concrete implementation faked it from fingertip position, and its own testers found consistent dynamics hard. So this one ships it coarse: peak approach speed, normalised per finger over a rolling window, in three bands — good for deliberate soft-vs-hard contrasts, not fine gradation. Fixed velocity mode (top button) turns it off entirely for an even, constant loudness.

Where the design comes from

  • The band itself — track everywhere, arm notes only in a zone: Leap's hover/touch zones, the WPI contactless-MIDI capstone's activation region, and the Stanford AR Piano's texture-space gating all do this split. Fifteen white keys is the evidenced sweet spot — wider keys beat more keys.
  • Per-finger polyphony — each fingertip evaluated independently against the key geometry, with its own active-note state (the WPI build's architecture). Black keys take priority where a fingertip overlaps both, as on a real piano.
  • Guide dots + hover light — Stanford drew coloured circles for every fingertip so players see where they'd land; hover feedback distinct from press feedback measurably cut errors in mid-air selection studies.
  • Colour keys — one colour per pitch class (the Chroma-Notes convention used in music teaching; ROLI's light-up keys do the same on hardware), with brightness carrying state: dim at rest, brighter on hover, full flare when sounding. The piano's black-key grouping stays, because every precedent that kept it played better.
  • Pinch-to-commit controls — the Quest / visionOS pattern: fingertip points, pinch commits, with a ~250 ms dwell and cooldowns against accidents. Vivomusicano runs the same hands-for-modes split in the browser on this exact stack.

Air Piano v1 — the third instrument in the gesture-music series, after Air Harp variations 1 and 2. It is designed to live as the air-piano sibling of the harp builds: same mirrored camera stage, same rule learned from the harp — selecting is silent; only a deliberate action plays — applied spatially, as a band you must reach and tap into. Expect the known limits: ring finger and pinky are the least accurately tracked fingers, hands crossing can swap labels, and slow chord playing will feel better than fast runs.
